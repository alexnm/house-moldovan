## The old capital

Kyoto is pretty much an essential stop for cultural tourism in Japan. The city was the imperial capital for more than a thousand years, and a lot of that history is still standing. If you arrive here from Tokyo or Osaka, Kyoto feels like a different country from many perspectives.

The city is famous for its Zen gardens, Buddhist temples, torii gates and traditional wooden houses. It is the perfect place to experience the roots of the Japanese culture, from the food and the tea ceremonies to the world of geishas.

You need around 3 or 4 days if you want to cover the main spots. Even though the city is not massive, its major attractions are spread around and public transportation is not as efficient as in Tokyo or Osaka. There are a few subway lines and a ton of regional trains to travel in and out of the city as well as an extensive bus network. Walking works well inside a single district, but plan a bus or train between them.

We visited during sakura, the cherry blossom season. However, the cherries in Kyoto were not in full bloom as they opened a bit later than expected that year. The weather was also not the friendliest, with rain here and there, but the city is beautiful no matter what.

## Buddhist temples

The two main religions in Japan are Buddhism and Shinto. Both are heavily represented with beautiful shrines and temples in Kyoto. The Buddhist temples have decorated gardens around them, which is part of why they are so popular in high season.

<Info>
  Temples in Japan are Buddhist, while shrines are for Shinto. However, it is
  very common for local people to pray both in temples and shrines, as they
  don't consider themselves strictly Buddhist or Shinto.
</Info>

Temples are recognized by the presence of **Buddha** statues, either outside or inside the main building. Shrines are more open and don't have large closed buildings. They are impossible to miss because of the **torii** gates — usually painted bright red — that you pass under on the way in.

**Kinkaku-ji**, the Golden Temple, is one of the most famous sights in Kyoto. It is situated in the western part, in an area well connected to the rest of the city by public transportation.

Its gold-leaf hall reflected in a pond, with a large open garden around it is the perfect representation of Zen Buddhism. It is busy and quite popular with tourists but doesn't require more than 30 minutes to an hour to visit, so it's a good place to start a day here.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-golden-temple-2.JPG"
  alt="Golden temple in the middle of a garden around a pond"
  caption="Kinkaku-ji, or the Golden Temple, is one of the most famous stops in Kyoto."
/>

**Ryōan-ji** is famous for its dry rock garden: a large rectangular area with white pebbles and fifteen stones arranged in five groups. Fun fact, the stones are arranged in such a way that you cannot see all of them from any single spot.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-ryoan-ji.JPG"
  alt="Stones arrangement over rows of white marble stones"
  caption="Beautiful garden arrangement at Ryōan-ji Temple."
/>

Because this one is close to Kinkaku-ji, it also attracts a lot of visitors. It is also quite small and tight, since access to the actual garden is limited and you only have a narrow hallway to walk back and forth.

Moving east now, **Ginkaku-ji**, or the Silver Pavilion, is a much quieter alternative for a similar size Buddhist temple. The really impressive feature of this temple is its meticulously designed garden. It has a lot of different paths criss-crossing the light green grass and moss fields.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-silver-temple-2.JPG"
  alt="Silver Pavilion framed by pines beside its pond"
  caption="Ginkaku-ji from the garden path."
/>

It is quite astonishing to learn how everything is planned to perfection inside these Zen gardens, while the end result looks like a 100% natural landscape. The garden also features a pond and it even has a raked gravel area, though much smaller than the one at Ryōan-ji.

South of Ginkaku-ji, the Philosopher's Path is a popular walking trail that follows the Lake Biwa Canal toward Nanzen-ji. It is a nice way to relax while walking along the canal, lined with cherry trees.

**Kiyomizu-dera** sits on a hillside in the east of the city, with a wooden stage looking out over the rooftops. It is also one of the most iconic sights in Kyoto, but unfortunately it was under heavy renovation during our visit.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-kiyomizu-dera-entrance.JPG"
  alt="Kiyomizu-dera entrance gate and stairs"
  caption="Kiyomizu-dera entrance gate."
/>

The temple is quite close to Gion, the famous geisha district. The lanes leading up to it are packed with shops and snack stalls. Give yourself time to wander those streets rather than treating the temple as a single stop.

And this is actually general advice for Kyoto. The city is full of beautiful temples, shrines and gardens, but the most interesting ones can be the ones you discover by accident.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-giouji-temple.JPG"
  alt="Moss garden and pale tree trunks at Giouji Temple"
  caption="The moss garden at Giouji, a quiet stop in Arashiyama."
/>

While we were in Arashiyama, we randomly stopped at **Giouji Temple**. It rarely shows up on tourist maps, but it featured one of the most beautiful moss gardens we've seen in Kyoto. And we were almost alone in the garden the entire time! Good luck finding your zen quiet spot at the famous temples and shrines.

Make time to wander around lesser-known temples. They usually offer a more relaxed and authentic visit, without the crowds and the noise. Most of these temples are open and free of charge, so don't hesitate to duck inside if you see something interesting from the outside.

## Fushimi Inari Taisha Shrine

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-fushimi-inari.JPG"
  alt="Looking through the red torii gates at Fushimi Inari"
  caption="It takes a lot of effort to take a picture with no one in the frame."
/>

The most popular spot in Kyoto is probably the **Fushimi Inari Taisha** shrine, famous for its endless torii gates covering the paths up and down the hill. It's a huge site covering an entire hillside, much bigger than any other temple or garden inside the city, so plan plenty of time if you want to enjoy the visit.

The torii gates are specific to Shinto shrines across Japan and usually mark entrances to sacred spaces or gardens. At Fushimi Inari however, there are several alleys up and down the hill that are covered in hundreds of torii gates, which makes this such a unique experience. As a side fact, the gates here are built as donations from businesses or wealthy families.

Fushimi Inari is popular all day, so expect a steady stream of people trying to take the perfect shot through the gates. The main shrine also attracts the most local people towards the bottom part of the hill. The crowds thin out as you climb. The upper trails and the small shrines near the summit are much quieter than the first few hundred gates.

<Info>
  Plan a few good hours up to half a day for Fushimi Inari. The site is huge and
  there are a lot of stairs. As you make your way to the top the crowds get
  thinner and the experience becomes more peaceful.
</Info>

We spent the afternoon there during one of our days in Kyoto and we stayed until sunset which gave us more time to enjoy the place without the huge crowds.

## Mount Kurama

If you spend an extra day in Kyoto or you just want to get off the beaten path, head out to **Mount Kurama**, in the north of the city. A local train runs from the city center into a heavily forested valley up north.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-kurama.jpg"
  alt="Stone stairs lined with red lanterns at Kurama"
  caption="The lantern-lined stairs on the way up Mount Kurama."
/>

You will find temples and shrines along a hiking trail up to **Kurama-dera**. The climb is a long stone stair lined with lanterns, through cedar forest. Going over the mountain, you can reach **Kifune Shrine**, another beautiful example of Shinto architecture, set along a river gorge.

<Info>
  Get some comfortable shoes as this is a proper forest walk, not a paved path
  in a park or a garden.
</Info>

## Arashiyama

In the western part of the city, **Arashiyama** is another very popular spot on the banks of the Katsura river. The main spotlight is the bamboo forest, a very picturesque spot with tall and dense bamboo trees. It's a haven for photographers and influencers alike.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-arashiyama-2.JPG"
  alt="A large path between bamboo trees"
  caption="Bamboo forest in Arashiyama."
/>

Unfortunately, the "forest" is quite small and it can get crowded during the day. It's best to arrive early in the morning or late in the afternoon if you want to avoid the swarms of tourists.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-arashiyama.JPG"
  alt="High and dense bamboo trees"
  caption="Bamboo forest in Arashiyama."
/>

As you cross the Katsura River, you can visit the Arashiyama Monkey Park. It sits on a hill and looks back over the city. You can spot the local **macaques** and buy bananas and other treats to feed them. These are wild animals, so keep your distance and follow the park's rules, they can be aggressive if they feel threatened.

## Gion

**Gion** is the famous geisha district of Kyoto. It has a unique atmosphere and is packed with visitors at all hours. The food in the area is excellent, and the neighbourhood still has beautiful wooden houses lined with craft shops. Late afternoon and evening is the best time to enjoy the atmosphere especially around the Hanamikoji pedestrian street.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-gion.jpg"
  alt="Lanterns on the streets of Gion"
  caption="Hanamikoji Street in Gion"
/>

Keep in mind that most of the ladies you see dressed in [traditional kimonos](https://theculturetrip.com/asia/japan/articles/a-brief-history-of-the-japanese-kimono/) are tourists, not geisha. Real geisha do not stroll the streets for photos. They are discreet, and you might only catch someone slipping toward a car or a restaurant.

Some of the best restaurants in Kyoto are located in small streets around Gion. Expect careful seasonal menus, tofu, pickles, and matcha, alongside the same sushi, noodles, and izakaya meals you find everywhere in Japan.

Very close to Gion, you can find the famous **Nishiki Market**, a covered arcade packed with stalls and vendors offering everything from street food to local specialties.

## Greater Kansai region

Kyoto works well as a base for the rest of Kansai. The city itself can easily fill three or four days, but there are plenty of day trips or half day trips you can take to explore the region.

**Osaka** is just 30-40 minutes away by train and it's a massive city that you can easily spend another day or two exploring. Having spent some time in Tokyo before coming here, we focused less on the big city experience and more on the culture and the temples, so we only had [half a day in Osaka](/spotlights/osaka-dotonbori).

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/osaka-dotonbori-3.JPG"
  alt="Dotonbori canal in central Osaka"
  caption="Dotonbori canal in central Osaka"
/>

**Nara** is another popular spot in the region. It is famous for its free-roaming deer in the large park area in the center of the city. It also has its fair share of cultural sights like the giant bronze Buddha at Tōdai-ji, and the lantern-lined walk up to Kasuga-taisha.

West of Kyoto, **Himeji Castle** is one of the finest examples of medieval castles still standing in the country while **Kobe** is an easy stop on the way back if you want to taste the famous beef.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/himeji-3.JPG"
  alt="Himeji Castle from a distance"
  caption="Himeji Castle from a distance."
/>

Travelling between these spots often feels like you never leave the city, as this is a heavily urbanized region. The [Keihanshin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keihanshin) metropolitan area (Osaka-Kobe-Kyoto) is actually the second largest metropolitan area in Japan, after Tokyo, home to around 20 million people.

## Travel Tips

Because we only had a vague idea of what to expect from Kyoto, we made a list of all the places we want to visit and placed them on a map. We then split the days based on covering short distances between spots.

For example, the Golden Temple (Kinkaku-ji) is quite close to Ryōan-ji, so it's easy to combine them. And they are both relatively close to the west part of the city and the Arashiyama bamboo forest. But don't try to plan for too many sights in one day, you will quickly get overwhelmed and you will miss the beauty of each spot.

There are plenty of other popular spots in Kyoto that are worth a visit, like the Nijo Castle or the Kyoto Imperial Palace and National Garden, so do your own research and figure out what you want to fit into your itinerary before you arrive.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-giouji-temple-2.JPG"
  alt="Moss garden and a gravel path at Giouji Temple"
  caption="The path along the moss garden at Giouji."
/>

Mornings are the best time to arrive at the big sites, as everywhere else where big crowds gather during the day. By late morning the paths fill up, especially at Fushimi Inari and Arashiyama, so keep that in mind.

Contrary to popular belief, Japan is still a cash-heavy country. We did visit Japan almost 10 years ago but we heard it hasn't changed much since then, except for the big places like Tokyo or Osaka. So it's best to carry cash with you for temple tickets and buses.

<AssetFigure
  path="japan/kyoto-golden-temple-3.JPG"
  alt="Focus on a branch of a pine tree in the foreground and the Golden Temple in the background"
  caption="Another view of Kinkaku-ji from across the pond."
/>

Because we wanted to visit more places around Kyoto, we based ourselves just outside the city, about halfway between Osaka and Kyoto. Inside the wider region, the [Kansai Area Pass](https://www.surutto.com/kansai_rwpl/en/) is an affordable multi-day ticket for trains, subways, and buses that's really worth it.
