## The old capital

Kyoto is famous for its temples and shrines, and it is pretty much an essential stop for cultural tourism in Japan. The city was the imperial capital for more than a thousand years, and a lot of that history is still standing — wooden lanes, walled gardens, and religious sites that feel a world away from Tokyo.

You need around 3–4 days if you want to cover the main spots. Even though the city is not that big, its major attractions are spread around, and public transportation is not as efficient as in Tokyo or Osaka. There are a few subway lines and also regional trains to travel outside the city. Walking works well inside a single district, but plan a bus or train between them.

We visited during sakura. The cherries in Kyoto were not in full bloom — they opened a bit later than expected that year — and the weather was not the friendliest, with rain here and there. It was still a great few days, and the gardens look beautiful even when the blossoms are only starting.

The two main religions in Japan are Buddhism and Shinto. Both are heavily represented with beautiful shrines and temples in Kyoto. The Buddhist temples have decorated gardens around them, which is part of why they are so popular in high season.

<Info type="tip">
  In short, temples are Buddhist, while shrines are Shinto. However, it is very
  common for local people to pray both in temples and shrines, as they don't
  consider themselves strictly Buddhist or Shinto.
</Info>

Temples are recognized by the presence of **Buddha** statues, either outside or inside the main building. Shrines are more open and don't have large closed buildings. They are impossible to miss because of the **torii** gates — usually painted bright red — that you pass under on the way in.

## How to spend the days

A simple split that worked for us: one day for the famous temples in the north and east, one long day for the hills (Kurama and Fushimi Inari), and one day for Arashiyama in the west plus an evening in Gion. If you only have two days, drop Kurama and keep Fushimi Inari, Kiyomizu-dera, and Arashiyama.

Mornings are the best time to arrive at the big sites. By late morning the paths fill up, especially at Fushimi Inari and the bamboo grove. Carry cash for temple tickets and buses — a lot of smaller places still prefer it.

We based ourselves just outside the city, about halfway between Osaka and Kyoto, and were usually 25 to 35 minutes from the center by local train. Inside the wider region, the [Kansai Pass](https://www.surutto.com/tickets/kansai_thru_english.html) is an affordable multi-day ticket for trains, subways, and buses.

## Buddhist temples

The Golden Temple, the Silver Temple, Kiyomizu-dera, and Ryōan-ji are some of the most popular spots you should not miss. They are all Buddhist temples, most of them belonging to the Zen branch of Buddhism.

**Kinkaku-ji**, the Golden Pavilion, is the postcard view: a gold-leaf hall reflected in a pond, with a garden path that loops around it. It is busy, and the visit itself is a walk rather than a long stay inside, but the first sight of the building is worth the trip across town.

**Ginkaku-ji**, the Silver Pavilion, is quieter in feel even when the path is crowded. The hall itself was never covered in silver. What you come for is the garden: a dark wooden pavilion beside a pond, a raked sand cone, and a walk up into the trees behind the temple. From here you can continue on the Philosopher's Path if you want a slower stretch between temples — a canal-side walk that is especially nice when the cherries are out.

**Kiyomizu-dera** sits on a hillside in the east of the city, with a wooden stage looking out over the rooftops. The lanes leading up to it are packed with shops and snack stalls. Give yourself time to wander those streets rather than treating the temple as a single stop.

**Ryōan-ji** is famous for its dry rock garden: a rectangle of white gravel and a careful arrangement of stones. Sit on the veranda for a few minutes. It is a different kind of visit from the gilded halls — small, quiet, and easy to rush past if you are ticking boxes.

Make time to wander around lesser-known temples as well. They usually offer a more relaxed and authentic visit, without the crowds and the noise. While we were in Arashiyama we randomly stopped at [Giouji Temple](https://goo.gl/maps/XXJb1AMS8CGPSSqHA). It rarely shows up on tourist maps, and the moss garden stopped us in our tracks. We are pretty sure this is not an exception — leave a gap in the schedule and duck into whichever gate looks quiet.

## Mount Kurama

If you spend that extra day in Kyoto, take a trip outside the city to **Mount Kurama**. A local train runs north into a heavily forested valley.

You will find temples and shrines along a hiking trail up to **Kurama-dera**. The climb is a long stone stair lined with lanterns, through cedar forest. Going over the mountain, you can reach **Kifune Shrine**, another beautiful example of Shinto architecture, set along a river gorge. It is a proper walk, not a quick photo stop, so wear shoes you can climb in and start early enough that you are not racing the last train.

## Fushimi Inari

The most visited spot in Kyoto is the **Fushimi Inari Taisha** shrine, famous for its endless torii gates covering the paths up and down the hill. The gates are donations from businesses, and they keep going long after the lower slopes — which is where most people turn around.

<Info type="warning">
  Plan enough time for Fushimi Inari. The site is huge, and there are a lot of
  stairs to go up and down.
</Info>

The whole mountain is popular all day, so expect a steady stream of people trying to take the perfect shot through the gates. The crowds thin as you climb. The upper trails and the small shrines near the summit are much quieter than the first few hundred gates.

We mixed Mount Kurama and Fushimi Inari on the same day, and it was long and exhausting. Leave real time between them if you want to enjoy either one.

## Arashiyama

In the western part of the city, the **Arashiyama** bamboo forest is another very picturesque spot. The main grove is short — a few minutes of walking if you go straight through — so arrive early, or accept that you will share the path. The surrounding lanes, the river, and the smaller temples nearby are what make it worth a half day.

Cross the Katsura River and you can enter the Arashiyama Monkey Park. It sits on a hill and looks back over the city. You can spot the local **macaques** and buy bananas and other treats to feed them. These are wild animals, so keep your distance and follow the park's rules.

## Gion

**Gion** is the famous geisha district. It has a unique atmosphere and is packed with visitors at all hours. The food in the area is excellent, and the neighbourhood still has beautiful wooden houses lined with craft shops. Evening is the time to walk it — the lanterns are on, and the side streets south of Shijō feel very different from the main shopping drag.

Keep in mind that most of the people you see dressed in [traditional kimonos](https://theculturetrip.com/asia/japan/articles/a-brief-history-of-the-japanese-kimono/) are tourists, not geisha. Real geisha do not stroll the streets for photos. They are discreet, and you might only catch someone slipping toward a car or a restaurant. Be respectful and skip the paparazzi routine. The [official travel guide](https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e3902.html) explains Gion well, including the difference between maiko and geiko.

## Food and evenings

Kyoto's cooking is quieter than Osaka's street food. Expect careful seasonal menus, tofu, pickles, and matcha, alongside the same sushi, noodles, and izakaya meals you find everywhere in Japan. We ate well just by walking into small places near where we happened to be — around Gion in the evening, and near the station when we were changing trains.

Nishiki Market is the obvious daytime stop if you want to graze: pickles, grilled seafood, and sweets along a covered arcade in the center. It gets very tight in the middle of the day, so an earlier visit is easier.

## Beyond the city

### Doing things around Kyoto

Kyoto works well as a base for the rest of Kansai. The city itself will fill three or four days, and the places around it are easy day trips by train.

**Nara** is the obvious one: free-roaming deer in the park, the giant bronze Buddha at Tōdai-ji, and the lantern-lined walk up to Kasuga-taisha. **Osaka** is the opposite mood — Dōtonbori at night, and a viewpoint over the city from Umeda. To the west, **Himeji Castle** is one of the finest medieval castles in the country, and **Kobe** is an easy stop on the way back if you want the famous beef and a walk by the port.

We did those as day trips while staying in the region, then continued north toward the Japanese Alps. The day-by-day plan, including how we split Kyoto, Nara, Osaka, and Himeji, is in our [two-week Japan itinerary](/itineraries/japan-cherry-blossom-2-weeks).
